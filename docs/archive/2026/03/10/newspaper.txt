* Tony Hoare’s Hidden Legacy: Beyond Quicksort and the Billion-Dollar Mistake
  http://lefenetrou.blogspot.com/2026/03/in-memoriam-tony-hoare.html
  While Hoare is widely remembered for quicksort and the 'null reference mistake,' his deeper contributions—**Communicating Sequential Processes (CSP)**, **Hoare Logic**, and the **Unifying Theories of Programming (UTP)**—laid the groundwork for modern concurrency, distributed systems, and formal verification. The community laments that these foundational (yet less celebrated) ideas shaped entire fields but remain overshadowed by his more famous work. His vision for rigorous, mathematically proven software persists as an unfulfilled but transformative ideal. Quote: "There are two ways of constructing a piece of software: One is to make it so simple there are obviously no deficiencies, and the other is to make it so complicated that there are no obvious deficiencies."

* The Meta-Game of Meaningful Research
  https://nicholas.carlini.com/writing/2026/how-to-win-a-best-paper-award.html
  While high-impact research is often framed as a matter of 'taste' or technical prowess, community discourse suggests it is equally driven by navigating the social architecture of academia—securing the right resources, mastering the art of 'killing' unproductive ideas, and understanding that 'important' work is frequently defined by the idiosyncrasies of conference culture. Quote: Find the resources, network, or skills that get you into the position of being able to work on the interesting research problem.

* Choosing the Right Tool for the Job: Pragmatic App Development in 2025
  https://arcanenibble.github.io/so-you-want-to-write-an-app.html
  The community highlights that the best tool for building an app depends on context—not dogma. For personal or niche use cases, simplicity and familiarity often trump modern trends: legacy tools (Lazarus, Tcl/Tk, even VB) still excel for desktop utilities, while web tech dominates for distribution and collaboration. The shift away from native apps isn’t about capability but *incentives*: universal access, zero-install updates, and shareability outweigh the declining innovation in native toolkits. The lesson? Match the tool to the problem’s scale, audience, and maintenance needs—whether that’s a decades-old language, a browser app, or a hybrid approach. Quote: "Increasingly, there just aren't enough incentives to write native apps."

* The Friction of Freedom
  https://www.smartlab.at/rss-revival-life-after-social-media/
  The barrier to an RSS renaissance isn't just a lack of awareness, but a fundamental trade-off: users must choose between the burden of manual curation and the bias of opaque algorithms. While RSS offers a sovereign feed, its mass adoption is hindered by the absence of a 'benevolent middleman' that balances cross-device sync with effective discovery. Quote: If you have to explain it, there is zero chance of massive adoption.

* The Deep Satisfaction—and Activism—of Repairing What We Own
  https://fixfest.therestartproject.org/
  Repairing broken items isn’t just practical—it’s emotionally rewarding, environmentally responsible, and increasingly a form of resistance against disposable culture. The community highlights the joy of reusing long-hoarded parts, the parallels between hardware repair and maintainable software (like open-source projects with good docs), and the irony that fixing what you own now feels like an act of activism. Stocking common repair supplies (e.g., capacitors, power cords) can empower local 'restart parties,' while the challenge of proprietary components (like USB-C ports) underscores systemic barriers to sustainability. Quote: "The fact that fixing what you own now requires 'activism' is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

* Legacy Systems and Real-Time Constraints: When Pragmatism Trumps Dogma
  https://old.reddit.com/r/emacs/comments/lly7po/comment/gnvzisy/
  The discussion highlights the tension between proven, adaptable tools (like Emacs) and the rigid demands of real-time systems. While Emacs' garbage collection and extensibility make it ill-suited for air traffic control's hard real-time requirements, the thread underscores a broader lesson: **legacy systems often persist not due to technical superiority, but because they *work reliably within their constraints*—and replacing them carries unmeasured risks**. The debate reveals how engineering trade-offs (e.g., stability vs. modernity) play out in critical infrastructure, where 'madness' to outsiders may reflect deep operational pragmatism. Quote: "EMACS' GC and 'real time ATC' concepts are absolutely antagonistic."
